# Manufacturing Lead Contact & Approach Strategy

## 1. Who to Contact First by Industry

### Plastic Injection Molding

**Primary target: VP Operations or Plant Manager.** In injection molding shops, the VP Ops or Plant Manager is the person who lives the daily pain of scrap rates, machine downtime, and quality escapes. They are the most receptive to solutions that promise visibility into production issues, because they are the ones woken up at 2am when a customer rejects a shipment. They also have budget authority for operational tools in the $5K-$50K range without needing board approval.

**Secondary target: Quality Manager/Director.** Quality managers in molding shops are under constant pressure from OEM customers demanding PPAP documentation, IATF 16949 compliance, and corrective action reports. They are natural champions for any tool that helps track and manage quality workflows. They tend to be detail-oriented and will want to see exactly how your system works before recommending it upward.

**When to go to the CEO/Owner:** At shops under 75 employees (Midwest Precision Molding, Ironwood Plastics, Precision Molded Plastics), the owner-operator IS the VP Ops. Go direct. At larger shops (Wabash Plastics, Dalal Plastics, Micro Plastics India), start with the operations or quality lead and ladder up.

### CNC Machining

**Primary target: Quality Assurance Director or Quality Manager.** CNC shops live and die by tolerances. When you are machining aerospace parts to sub-micron precision (Owens Industries, HPPI, LeanWerks), a single out-of-spec part can trigger a $500K customer audit. The QA Director is the most pain-aware buyer and the fastest path to a trial. Todd Gruchalski at Panek Precision and Paul Gilson at Swiss-Tech are ideal first contacts.

**Secondary target: VP of Sales or Business Development.** In CNC shops, the sales/BD leader often drives technology adoption because they need to win new certifications (AS9100, NADCAP, CMMC) to land bigger contracts. If your platform helps them demonstrate quality management capabilities during customer audits, they become strong internal advocates. Jay Grizzle at Panek Precision and Dan Schneider at Trace-A-Matic are good examples.

**When to go to the CEO/Owner:** At founder-led shops (Reid & Suzanna Leland at LeanWerks, Andrew Magiera at HyTech), the founder is hands-on and makes fast decisions. At family shops (Panek, Moseys'), the patriarch may need to be involved for any purchase, but approach through the quality or ops lead first.

### Metal Stamping & Forming

**Primary target: VP Operations or COO.** Stamping operations are high-volume, high-speed production environments where a single die crash or material variance can halt an entire production line. The VP Ops or COO (Donna Brand at Keats, Donald Connerton at OGS, Michael Steger at Waukesha Metal, Ben Barnett at Principal) feels this pain most acutely and has the authority to trial new tools. Stamping COOs tend to be more hands-on than their counterparts in other industries.

**Secondary target: Quality Manager.** With IATF 16949 being the table-stakes certification in automotive stamping, quality managers are perpetually swamped with corrective action requests, customer audits, and PPAP submissions. John Scheftic at OGS and Kushal Nikam at Protech Stampings are ideal quality-focused entry points.

**When to go to the CEO/Owner:** Small family-owned stampers (Graham Stamping, Henderson Stamping, ESI) have owner-operators who handle operations, quality, and sales themselves. Clay Porter at Henderson, David MacHarg at Graham, and Ron Delfini at ESI are all directly approachable.

### Food & Beverage Manufacturing

**Primary target: VP Operations or Plant Manager.** Food manufacturing operations must balance production efficiency with strict food safety requirements. The VP Ops is the person who juggles HACCP plans, allergen controls, sanitation schedules, and production output simultaneously. Wade Fitzkee at G&S Foods and Jeff Liddy at The Father's Table are classic VP Ops targets.

**Secondary target: Food Safety/Quality Manager.** Food safety managers live in constant fear of recalls, FDA warning letters, and audit failures. The Father's Table received an FDA warning letter in November 2025 for Listeria — their quality team is now hyper-alert. Sandra Duhaney at Caribbean Food Delights and Francis Johnston at DeLallo are quality-focused entry points. In India, Pratiksha Sonawane at Varun Agro and Vishal Sharma at Prataap Snacks fill this role.

**When to go to the CEO/Owner:** At family-run food companies (Beaverton Foods, DeLallo, Mala's, Vasant Masala, Mapro), the family patriarch often makes final purchasing decisions. But they rely heavily on their operations and quality teams for recommendations. In India especially, approach the Managing Director directly at smaller companies — Manisha Dhatrak at Varun Agro (woman-founded, likely receptive to modern tools) or Chandrakant Bhandari at Vasant Masala.


## 2. The Decision-Making Chain in Manufacturing Companies

Manufacturing purchasing decisions follow a predictable ladder. Understanding it prevents wasted effort on contacts who cannot buy.

**Tier 1 — Problem Owner (Quality Manager, Plant Manager):** This person experiences the daily pain your product solves. They can request budget and initiate a vendor evaluation, but rarely have unilateral purchasing authority above $5K-$10K. Their power is the power of recommendation. Win them, and they sell internally for you.

**Tier 2 — Budget Holder (VP Operations, COO, VP Manufacturing):** This person owns the P&L for the plant or operations function. They approve purchases in the $10K-$100K range. They care about ROI, payback period, and integration with existing systems (ERP, MES, QMS). They will ask "How does this reduce scrap?" or "Will this cut our corrective action response time?" Have numbers ready.

**Tier 3 — Final Approver (CEO/Owner, CFO, President):** At companies under 200 employees, this person is involved in every purchase over $25K. At family-owned companies (common in all four industries), the founder or patriarch may weigh in even on smaller purchases. They care about competitive advantage, risk mitigation, and whether the tool will still be relevant in 3-5 years. They do not want a demo; they want a one-page summary of what it does and what it costs.

**Typical timeline:** From first contact to signed contract, manufacturing companies take 60-120 days for a $10K-$50K solution. The cycle extends to 4-6 months for $50K+ deals. Indian manufacturing companies often move faster on decisions (30-60 days) because the MD typically has more centralized authority.


## 3. Multi-Threading Accounts: Contacting Multiple People at the Same Company

Multi-threading is the single most effective way to increase win rates in manufacturing sales. Here is how to do it without creating internal confusion.

**The 2+1 Rule:** For every target account, contact at least two operational contacts and one executive. The operational contacts build ground-level support; the executive contact ensures awareness at the decision-making level.

**Sequencing matters:**

1. **Week 1:** Send the first email to the Quality Manager or Plant Manager. This is a peer-level, problem-focused message. Reference a specific challenge relevant to their role (e.g., "Managing corrective actions across multiple shifts" or "Tracking supplier quality metrics for IATF audits").

2. **Week 2:** Send to the VP Operations or COO. This message is outcome-focused: cost savings, efficiency gains, audit readiness. Do NOT reference that you also emailed the Quality Manager — let the threads converge naturally.

3. **Week 3:** If neither has responded, send to the CEO/Owner with a very brief, high-level message. If one of the first two responded, hold off on the CEO contact — you do not need top-down pressure when you already have a champion.

**For accounts with confirmed multi-person contact data (examples from our database):**

- **EMC Precision:** Email Bob Graney (CEO) + Luke Wright (VP Plant Manager Ohio) + Jeff Ohlemacher (Chairman). Three different angles: growth strategy (CEO), operational efficiency (Plant Manager), strategic alignment (Chairman).

- **OGS Industries:** Email John Scheftic (Quality Manager) first, then Donald Connerton (COO), then Kevin Bader (CEO). Quality → Operations → Executive ladder.

- **Tulkoff Food Products:** Email Rodney Hopkins (COO) first, then Mike Kagan (CEO). Operations pain → executive confirmation.

**Critical rule:** Never CC multiple contacts at the same company on a cold email. Each person should feel the outreach is personal and specific to their role.


## 4. Warm-Up Tactics Before the Cold Email

Cold emails to manufacturing executives convert at 1-3%. Warm emails convert at 8-15%. Here is how to warm up each account before the first outreach.

### LinkedIn Engagement (2-3 weeks before email)

1. **Follow the company page.** Like and comment on 2-3 of their recent posts. Manufacturing companies post infrequently, so your engagement stands out.

2. **Connect with the target contact on LinkedIn.** Use a connection note referencing something specific: "Saw your presentation at [trade show]" or "Congrats on the AS9100 certification" or "Noticed your expansion into [new market]."

3. **Engage with their content.** If they share anything about quality challenges, operational improvements, or industry trends, leave a thoughtful comment. Manufacturing professionals get very little LinkedIn engagement, so your comment will be noticed and remembered.

### Industry-Specific Warm-Up Signals

**Plastic Injection Molding:**
- Reference their specific certifications (IATF 16949 for automotive suppliers, ISO 13485 for medical)
- Mention reshoring trends and capacity challenges — this is top-of-mind for US molders in 2025-2026
- For India companies, reference export quality standards and customer audit preparation

**CNC Machining:**
- Reference CMMC compliance requirements (LeanWerks is actively pursuing Level 2)
- Mention AS9100 audit preparation or NADCAP requirements
- For aerospace shops, reference supply chain quality requirements from Boeing/Airbus/Lockheed

**Metal Stamping & Forming:**
- Reference die maintenance and predictive quality challenges
- Mention IATF 16949 customer-specific requirements (every automotive stamper deals with these)
- For companies like Highco Engineers or Devchhaya Group, reference Tata Motors quality standards

**Food & Beverage Manufacturing:**
- Reference recent FDA enforcement actions or recalls in their category
- The Father's Table received an FDA warning letter in Nov 2025 — their competitors are now on high alert
- For Indian companies, reference FSSAI compliance updates or BRC audit preparation
- Mention SQF certification journey (Caribbean Food Delights achieved Level 3, which is rare)

### Content-Based Warm-Up

Before reaching out, share a piece of useful content with no ask attached. Options include:

- A one-page industry benchmark report (e.g., "Average scrap rates in injection molding by part complexity")
- A checklist relevant to an upcoming audit cycle
- A short case study from a similar company (anonymized if needed)
- A news article about their industry with a brief personal note

The goal is to establish yourself as a useful resource, not a salesperson, before the first sales email lands.


## 5. Best Days and Times to Send for Manufacturing

Manufacturing executives operate on plant schedules, not office schedules. Their day starts early and their inbox time is limited.

### Optimal Send Windows

**Best days:** Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday. Manufacturing plants run Monday through Friday (sometimes Saturday). Monday mornings are consumed by production meetings and reviewing weekend issues. Friday afternoons are mentally checked out.

**Best time: 5:30 AM - 6:30 AM local time.** This is the golden window. Manufacturing executives and plant managers typically arrive at 6:00-6:30 AM, before the first shift starts at 7:00 AM. They check email during this quiet window before the floor demands their attention. An email that arrives at 5:45 AM sits at the top of their inbox when they open it.

**Second-best time: 7:30 PM - 8:30 PM local time.** After dinner, many manufacturing leaders do a final email check. The volume of competing emails is low.

**Worst time: 9:00 AM - 4:00 PM.** Once the plant is running, the VP Ops and Plant Manager are on the floor handling production issues, meeting with supervisors, and dealing with customer calls. Email goes unread until the plant quiets down.

### Time Zone Considerations

**US Companies:** The database spans Eastern, Central, Mountain, and Pacific time zones. Segment your sends accordingly:

- Eastern (OGS Industries, Weiss-Aug, ESI, Tulkoff, Caribbean Food Delights): Send at 5:30 AM ET
- Central (Keats, Principal Manufacturing, Wabash Plastics, Henderson Stamping, G&S Foods): Send at 5:30 AM CT
- Mountain (LeanWerks, HPPI): Send at 5:30 AM MT
- Pacific (Owens Industries, Moseys', Medway, Trademark Plastics, Beaverton Foods): Send at 5:30 AM PT

**India Companies:** Indian manufacturing typically runs 9:00 AM - 6:00 PM IST. The best send window is 8:00-8:30 AM IST (before the workday begins). India has a single time zone (IST, UTC+5:30), so one send time covers all India contacts.

### Industry-Specific Timing Nuances

**Plastic Injection Molding:** Many shops run 24/7 with 3 shifts. The day-shift management team arrives at 6:00-6:30 AM to review overnight production. Send emails to arrive by 5:45 AM.

**CNC Machining:** Aerospace-focused shops (Swiss-Tech, LeanWerks, Moseys') often run single-shift or extended-day operations (6 AM - 6 PM). Quality managers in these shops tend to arrive early and review inspection reports. Send by 5:30 AM.

**Metal Stamping:** High-volume stamping operations (Waukesha Metal, OGS, Keats) run 2-3 shifts. Plant managers and quality staff overlap between day and evening shifts around 2:30-3:30 PM. A second-touch email sent at 3:00 PM can catch them during shift transition when they are at their desks rather than on the floor.

**Food & Beverage:** Food plants often start production at 4:00-5:00 AM due to fresh ingredient deliveries and temperature requirements. Management arrives by 6:00 AM. Send emails at 5:15-5:30 AM. Avoid sending during major food industry events: SQF Annual Conference (usually May), IFT Food Expo (usually July), and GFSI Conference (usually February-March).

### Sequence Cadence

For a standard 5-touch cold email sequence, use this manufacturing-optimized cadence:

- **Email 1:** Tuesday at 5:30 AM local time
- **Email 2:** Thursday at 5:30 AM (same week, 2 days later)
- **Email 3:** Tuesday at 7:30 PM (following week)
- **Email 4:** Thursday at 5:30 AM (same week as Email 3)
- **Email 5:** Tuesday at 5:30 AM (two weeks after Email 4) — breakup email

Total sequence duration: approximately 3.5 weeks. Manufacturing decision-makers need more time between touches than SaaS buyers because they are physically away from email for most of the day.

### Seasonal Considerations

**Avoid:** The last two weeks of December (plant shutdowns), the first week of January (restart chaos), the week of major industry trade shows (NPE for plastics, IMTS for machining/stamping, Pack Expo for food).

**Best months for outreach:** February, March, September, October. These are the months when manufacturing companies are actively planning budgets and evaluating new vendors. January is budget-setting month (too early for new vendor conversations). April-May is audit season (quality managers are unavailable). June-August has vacation-related gaps. November-December is year-end closeout.
