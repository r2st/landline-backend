## Part 1: Cold Calling Fundamentals

### 1.1 The Purpose of a Cold Call

The sole purpose of a cold call is to earn a meeting — not to sell, not to pitch, not to qualify. Everything on the call serves one objective: get the prospect to agree to a 15–30 minute conversation at a specific date and time. (Schiffman, Weinberg, Blount)

### 1.2 The Math That Drives Everything

**Activity-to-Revenue Formula (Schiffman's A=P=S):**

| Metric | Target |
|--------|--------|
| Dials per day | 40–60 |
| Conversations per day | 8–12 |
| Appointments set per day | 1–2 |
| Appointments per week | 5–8 |
| Close rate on qualified meetings | 15–25% |
| Deals per month | 3–6 |

**The 30-Day Rule (Blount):** The prospecting you do in any 30-day window pays off over the next 90 days. Skip a month of prospecting and you'll feel the pipeline drought three months later. This is non-negotiable — the pipeline doesn't empty overnight, it empties silently.

**The Law of Replacement (Blount):** Every closed deal removes multiple prospects from your pipeline. You must replace at a rate equal to or greater than your close rate, or the pipeline shrinks.

### 1.3 Best Times to Call Manufacturing Decision Makers

| Time Window | Why It Works | Best For |
|-------------|-------------|----------|
| 6:30–8:00 AM | Before meetings start; plant managers at desk before walking the floor | Plant Managers, VP Ops |
| 10:00–11:30 AM | Past morning rush, before lunch; generally available | All roles |
| 2:00–3:30 PM | Post-lunch, before end-of-shift; good secondary window | All roles |
| 4:00–5:00 PM | Decision-makers wrapping up day; 47% higher connect rate (Gong data) | Executives, Procurement |

**Best days:** Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday

**Avoid:** Monday before 10 AM (inbox overload), Friday after 3 PM (weekend mode)

**Manufacturing-specific timing:** Call during shift change windows (6:30–8 AM or 2–4 PM) when plant managers are most likely at their desks, not on the floor.

**Industry-Specific Timing Notes:**

- Injection molding and stamping plants often run multiple shifts. Try calling between shifts (6:00–7:00 AM, 2:00–3:00 PM) to catch supervisors during transition.
- Food and beverage plants with USDA or FDA inspectors on-site may have specific windows. Early morning (before inspectors arrive) and late afternoon (after inspections) tend to be best.
- CNC job shops often have owners who arrive early (6:30–7:30 AM) and stay late. The early morning window is often the most productive.

**Holiday and seasonal considerations:** Avoid plant shutdown periods — the week of July 4th, the last two weeks of December. Many plants also shut down for a week in summer. Connect rates drop 40–60% during these windows.

### 1.4 Pre-Call Research — The Smart Calling Framework (Sobczak)

Never dial without intelligence. The difference between a "cold" call and a "smart" call is 5 minutes of research.

**Research checklist:**

- [ ] Check LinkedIn for the contact's recent posts and job history
- [ ] Read their company job postings — hiring signals reveal priorities and budget activity
- [ ] Search Google News for recent company announcements
- [ ] Check their website for capabilities, certifications, equipment lists
- [ ] Look for trigger events: expansions, certifications, new customers, equipment purchases
- [ ] Use ThomasNet to understand production capabilities
- [ ] Identify mutual connections or referral paths

**Set Primary and Secondary Objectives (Sobczak):**

- Primary: Schedule a 15-minute meeting this week
- Secondary (minimum acceptable outcome): Get a name/referral, permission to send one page, a scheduled callback date

You never "fail" a call if you achieve the secondary objective.

## Part 2: Opening Scripts — Seven Proven Frameworks

### 2.1 The Blount 5-Step Opening

**Structure:** Attention → Identify → Reason → Bridge → Ask

> "[First Name]. [Pause.] This is [Your Name] with [Company]. The reason I'm calling is that [bridge — trigger event, relevant pain, result for similar company]. I'd like to set up 15 minutes to see if what we did for them could be relevant for your operation. Are you available Thursday afternoon or Friday morning?"

**Manufacturing example (injection molding):**

> "Dave. This is [Name] with [Company]. The reason I'm calling is that I saw [Company] is expanding into medical molding — congratulations on that. We've been working with a couple of other shops making that transition, and the biggest challenge they hit was getting their quality documentation up to FDA standards without slowing down production. I'd love to share what worked for them — are you available for 15 minutes Thursday or Friday?"

### 2.2 The Schiffman Third-Party Endorsement

**Structure:** Greeting → Identify → Reason (specifically) → Social Proof → Ask

> "Good morning [First Name], this is [Your Name] from [Company]. The reason I'm calling you today specifically is that we just completed a project for [Known Client] that [specific result with metric]. What I'd like to do is stop by next [day] to walk you through what we did. How's [time]?"

**Manufacturing example (CNC machining):**

> "Good morning Jim, this is [Name] from [Company]. The reason I'm calling you today specifically is that we just completed a project for [Regional Machining Company] that reduced their scrap rate by 35% on close-tolerance aluminum parts. I'd like to stop by Thursday to walk you through what we did. How does 10am sound?"

### 2.3 The Weinberg Mini-Sales Story

**Structure:** Introduction → Bridge → Mini-Story (30–60 sec) → Meeting Request (using "Visit, Fit, Value")

> "Hi [Name], it's [Your Name] from [Company]. I head up [role/function]. Let me take a minute — we work with [type of buyer] who are [emotionally charged problem 1], dealing with [problem 2], or looking for [problem 3]. I'd love to visit with you for 30 minutes to see if there's any fit. I promise you'll walk away with value and ideas even if we're not a match."

**Key delivery rules:** Do NOT ask "Is this a good time?" (invites easy no). Do NOT use a "sales voice." Sound like a peer, an executive, a consultant.

**Manufacturing example (metal stamping):**

> "Hi Karen, it's [Name] from [Company]. I head up our partnerships with automotive stamping suppliers in the Midwest. Let me take a minute — we work with quality teams that are exhausted from manual SPC documentation, fed up with PPAP packages that take two days to assemble, or under pressure from OEM customers demanding real-time quality data. I'd love to visit with you for 30 minutes to see if there's a fit. You'll walk away with ideas even if we're not a match."

### 2.4 The Sobczak Smart Call Opening

**Structure:** Identify → Intelligence Connection → Possible Benefit → Second Benefit → Questions

> "Hi [First Name], I'm [Your Name] with [Company]. I [intelligence reference — saw, noticed, read about]. I work with [type of company] going through [similar situation], and we've helped them [specific result]. Depending on your specific situation, I might have a few ideas worth sharing — could I ask you a couple of questions?"

**Manufacturing example (food & beverage):**

> "Hi Sarah, I'm [Name] with [Company]. I saw that [Company] just completed your SQF recertification — congratulations. I work with food manufacturers going through that process, and one thing that keeps coming up is the documentation burden around lot-level traceability. We helped a similar operation cut their trace-back time from 48 hours to under 4. Depending on where you are with FSMA 204 prep, I might have some ideas — could I ask you a couple of questions?"

### 2.5 The Challenger Insight-Led Opening (Dixon & Adamson)

**Structure:** Credibility Hook → Reframe/Insight → Data Point → Ask

> "Most [role] I talk to are focused on [known problem]. What they tend to miss is [specific counterintuitive insight] — and when we dig into the numbers, the cost is usually [specific impact]. That's the piece that's actually worth fixing. Worth two minutes?"

**Manufacturing example (injection molding):**

> "Most purchasing managers I talk to in the molding space are focused on cycle time optimization to reduce cost per part. What they tend to miss is that mold maintenance scheduling has 3x more impact on overall cost-per-part than cycle time tweaks. When we dig into the numbers, inconsistent PM schedules are costing shops 8–12% of runtime without anyone tracking it. Worth two minutes?"

### 2.6 The Voss Accusation Audit Opening

**Structure:** No-Oriented Question → Accusation Audit → Relevance Statement → Ask

> "Is now a bad time? [They say no.] You're probably thinking this is just another vendor call — and you've probably heard exactly this pitch before. Fair enough. I work with [type of company] dealing with [specific problem], and I just want two minutes to find out if what I'm seeing at similar companies is even relevant to your situation."

**Manufacturing example (CNC machining):**

> "Is now a bad time? You're probably thinking I'm calling to tell you we can machine parts better than your current shop. You've heard that before and it didn't mean much. I'm not going to do that. I work with aerospace and defense OEMs that are dealing with inconsistent FAI documentation from their machining suppliers — and I just want to find out if that's something you're dealing with. Fair enough?"

### 2.7 The "Cold Calling Sucks" Transparency Opening (Farrokh & Cegelski)

**Structure:** Transparency → Permission → Reason → Question

> "Hey [Name], I know this is a cold call — I'll be quick. I work with [industry] companies dealing with [specific pain]. Is that something on your radar, or am I way off base?"

This radical transparency works especially well with manufacturing buyers who are deeply suspicious of vendor calls. Based on 300M+ Gong-analyzed calls.

## Part 3: Gatekeeper Strategies

### 3.1 Principles

Gatekeepers have seen every manipulation tactic. What works is respect, honesty, and making them an ally rather than an obstacle. They talk to the decision maker — how they describe you matters.

### 3.2 Five Strategies

**Strategy 1 — The Ally Approach:**

> "Hi, this is [Name] from [Company]. I'm hoping to reach [Decision Maker] — we work with manufacturing operations on [specific issue] and I wanted to see if it's relevant to what they're dealing with. Is [he/she] available?"

**Strategy 2 — The Help Request (Konrath):**

> "I know [his/her] time is extremely limited — would it be better to try to reach [him/her] early morning or later in the afternoon?"

Often reveals scheduling patterns and turns the gatekeeper into an advisor.

**Strategy 3 — The Schiffman Pattern Interrupt:**

When the gatekeeper says the decision maker is unavailable: "Can you and I meet?" No one says this. The gatekeeper is confused, typically says "Well, no — you need to speak with [Name]." You say: "Is she there?" — converting a dismissal into a live conversation.

**Strategy 4 — The Intelligence Gather (Sobczak):**

> "I understand [he/she] is busy. I'm going to be reaching out again and I want to make sure what I have is relevant. Could I ask you a couple of quick questions about how your company handles [specific function]?"

Gatekeepers often know more about the organization's priorities than you'd expect. Call the admin line, explain you want your call to be relevant, ask who handles vendor decisions for X.

**Strategy 5 — The Referral Request (Ross):**

> "I'm not sure if [Decision Maker] is the right person for this. Could you help me figure out who handles decisions about [specific function] at [Company]?"

Less threatening and often gets you to the actual decision maker.

### 3.3 What NOT to Do

- Don't pretend to know the decision maker personally
- Don't be vague about who you are or why you're calling
- Don't use manipulative tactics ("I'm returning [his/her] call")
- Don't treat them as obstacles — they talk to the decision maker
- Don't be rude or dismissive if they block you

### 3.4 Best Times to Bypass Gatekeepers

- Before 8:00 AM — executives often answer their own phones before admin staff arrives
- After 5:00 PM — same reason
- During lunch (12:00–1:00 PM) — admin coverage is thinnest
- Early Tuesday or Wednesday mornings — admin staff may be in meetings

## Part 4: Voicemail Strategies

### 4.1 The Blount 5-Step Voicemail Framework

1. Identify yourself upfront (name + company immediately, signals confidence)
2. Give your number immediately — say it twice, slowly
3. Give them a reason to call back (curiosity hook — a result for a similar company, a trigger event)
4. Keep it under 30 seconds — no pitching in voicemail
5. Repeat name and number at end

### 4.2 Voicemail Templates by Vertical

**Injection Molding:**

> "Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Company], [phone number]. We just helped a molder in [region] cut their scrap rate from 4.2% to 1.8% by changing how they track cavity-level data. I'm curious whether that kind of improvement is relevant for [Company]. My number again is [phone number]. Have a great day."

**CNC Machining:**

> "Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Company], [phone number]. I saw [Company] is doing a lot of AS9100 work — one shop we work with cut their first-article inspection time from 4 hours to 90 minutes. If that's interesting, I'd love to spend 10 minutes sharing how they did it. Again, [phone number]."

**Metal Stamping:**

> "Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Company], [phone number]. One of our stamping customers in [region] extended their average die life by 22% by moving from calendar-based to data-driven maintenance. Wanted to see if that's a challenge you're dealing with. My number is [phone number]."

**Food & Beverage:**

> "Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Company], [phone number]. With the FSMA 204 traceability requirements rolling out, I've been talking with a lot of food manufacturers about lot-level tracking challenges. One of them went from 48-hour trace-back to under 4 hours. If that's on your radar, I'd love to share what they did. Again, [phone number]."

### 4.3 Voicemail Rules

- Under 30 seconds — every time
- Never pitch your product in voicemail
- Lead with a result, not your company description
- Say your phone number slowly and clearly — twice
- Vary the message each call. Repeating the same message = permanent filter
- Follow every voicemail immediately with a brief email referencing it (the Double Play)

## Part 5: Objection Handling — Complete Framework

### 5.1 The Three Types of Resistance (Blount)

1. **Reflex Responses** — knee-jerk, automatic ("I'm busy," "Not interested"). Not real objections. 49.5% of all cold call resistance falls here (Gong-validated).
2. **Brush-Offs** — conflict avoidance ("Send me information," "Call back later"). Designed to end the call without confrontation.
3. **Real Objections** — considered barriers ("We already have a supplier," "No budget"). Rare in prospecting calls.

### 5.2 The Turnaround Framework: Anchor → Disrupt → Ask (Blount)

1. **Anchor** — acknowledge without fighting. Absorbs the resistance without reacting defensively.
2. **Disrupt** — break their expected pattern. They expect fight or fold; you do neither.
3. **Ask** — redirect to your specific, low-stakes objective.

**Two-RBO Rule:** If you hit two failed turnarounds in a row, disengage gracefully. "I understand. I'll reach back out in a few weeks — have a great day." Never push past two.

### 5.3 The Weinberg Three-Ask Rule

The first "no" is automatic and reflexive. Make three asks:

1. **Ask #1:** Request the meeting
2. **Ask #2:** If refused, acknowledge and re-ask: "Visit with me anyway."
3. **Ask #3 (The Money Line):** "[Name], I understand you're [objection]. Visit with me anyway. I promise you'll get value and ideas from our time, even if we end up not being a fit."

A salesperson who switched from accepting the first "no" to making three asks tripled their prospecting success rate.

### 5.4 The Ledge Technique (Schiffman)

Use the prospect's own first objection as a foothold to redirect toward scheduling: "You know something? That's exactly why we should get together." Shifts from "if" to "when."

### 5.5 Manufacturing-Specific Objection Scripts

**"We already have a supplier"**

> "That's great — I'd expect that for a plant your size. I'm not trying to replace that relationship. What I usually find is there's one part of the work that's a pain point — whether it's lead time on a specific material, quality issues on a particular job, or pricing on low-volume orders. Is there anything like that you're dealing with right now?"

Alternative (Backup Source approach):

> "That makes total sense. Most of our customers had a primary supplier when I first called. What I'd love to do is become your backup — so when your primary shop is at capacity or you need a second quote, you already know who to call. Can we spend 15 minutes so I understand your typical requirements?"

**"Not interested"**

> "Totally fair. Most of the plant managers I work with said the same thing on the first call — until a quality miss or a delivery problem put them in a tough spot. I just want to make sure you know who to call when that happens. Can I send you one page on what we specialize in?"

**"We're not looking right now"**

> "I hear you — timing is everything. Out of curiosity, when does your vendor review cycle come around? I'd love to be in the conversation when it does."

**"Send me some information"**

> "Happy to. To make sure I send you the most relevant stuff — can I ask what type of materials or part geometries take up most of your shop's capacity right now?"

Forces engagement or exposes the brush-off. If they engage, you've started a real conversation.

**"Your price is too high"**

> "I hear that a lot before we actually run numbers together. The comparison usually looks different once we account for scrap, rework, and freight costs from the current setup. Could we do a quick cost-per-part comparison on one of your current jobs?"

**"We're on an approved vendor list only"**

> "Understood. What's your process for adding a new supplier? We're ISO 9001 certified and can usually complete your qualification process within [X] weeks."

**"I don't have time right now"**

> "That's exactly why I kept this short. When would be a better time for a 10-minute call — Thursday morning or Friday afternoon?"

**"We're happy with everything"**

> "That's great to hear. Most of the shops I work with said the same thing — until their biggest customer audited them, or their main supplier couldn't handle a rush order. Can I ask — what would you do if your primary supplier had a 3-week delay on a critical order?"

**"I need to talk to my [boss/partner/team]"**

> "Absolutely — that makes sense. What information would be most helpful for that conversation? I can put together a one-page summary tailored to what [they] would want to see."

**"We tried this before and it didn't work"**

> "I hear that more than you'd think, and honestly, a lot of early tools for manufacturing weren't ready for prime time. What specifically didn't work? [Listen.] That's really helpful context. The reason we built this specifically for manufacturing is exactly because of stories like yours."

## Part 6: Multi-Channel Orchestration

### 6.1 The COMBO Framework (Hughes)

Single-channel outreach is dead. The building block is the Triple — phone + voicemail + email in under 2 minutes.

**The Triple:**

1. Call the prospect's direct line
2. Leave a voicemail immediately after
3. Send a personalized email referencing the voicemail

30 Triples per day = 2 disciplined "Hours of Power."

**Scaling the combo:**

- Triple = Call + Voicemail + Email
- Quad = Triple + LinkedIn InMail or connection request
- Quint = Quad + another social touch

### 6.2 The Email + Voicemail Double Play (Blount)

Follow every voicemail immediately with a brief email referencing it:

**Voicemail:**

> "Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Company]. We helped a [industry] plant in your area cut reject rates by 40%. I'm curious if that's relevant to what you're seeing at [Company]. I'll shoot you a quick email with the details — my number is [phone]."

**Immediate email:**

> Subject: Just left you a voicemail
>
> [First Name],
>
> Just tried you — wanted to share how [similar company] reduced their reject rates by 40% last quarter. Thought it might be relevant given [trigger/observation about their company].
>
> Worth a quick call this week?
>
> [Signature]

### 6.3 The 12-Touch Manufacturing Cadence

| Touch | Day | Channel | Content |
|-------|-----|---------|---------|
| 1 | 1 | Email | 3-sentence: Trigger → Pain → Proof → Question CTA |
| 2 | 2 | LinkedIn | Connection request referencing email |
| 3 | 3 | Phone + VM | Call referencing both email and LinkedIn connection |
| 4 | 5 | Email | Value-add: case study from peer manufacturer |
| 5 | 7 | LinkedIn | DM with question about their process (if connected) |
| 6 | 9 | Phone | Second call; reference LinkedIn; ask about specific challenge |
| 7 | 11 | Email | Different angle: address a common industry objection |
| 8 | 14 | Video Email | Personalized 90-second Loom with their website on screen |
| 9 | 17 | Direct Mail | Dimensional mailer with branded ops item + handwritten note |
| 10 | 20 | Phone | "We sent something your way — did it arrive?" |
| 11 | 24 | Email | Social proof: customer quote from peer manufacturer |
| 12 | 28 | Email | Break-up: "Should I close your file?" |

**Post-cadence:** Enter 60-day nurture (one value-add email every 3 weeks, no ask). Re-launch full cadence at 90 days with new angle.

### 6.4 LinkedIn Pre-Call Warming Sequence

Use LinkedIn to make your cold call feel warm:

1. **Day -5:** View their profile (they get notified)
2. **Day -3:** Like or comment substantively on one of their posts
3. **Day -1:** Send connection request with personalized note (no pitch)
4. **Day 0:** Make the phone call — your name is now recognized

Drives connection acceptance from ~30% to 45%+ and significantly improves cold call reception.

### 6.5 Trigger-Based Cadence Modifications

**Tier 1 Triggers — Act within 24–48 hours:**

- Leadership change (new VP/Director)
- Quality failure or rejected shipment from current supplier
- Job posting for roles your solution enables
- Competitor churn

**Tier 2 Triggers — Act within 1 week:**

- Prospect opened your email 3+ times without replying → escalate to phone
- Prospect clicked a link → personalize next touch to that content
- Prospect viewed your LinkedIn profile → connect immediately
- Intent data surge on your category → compress cadence timing

**Break the cadence when:**

- Prospect replies (move to conversation)
- Prospect books a meeting (exit all automated touches)
- Prospect changes jobs (pause 30 days; re-engage at new company)

## Part 7: Discovery Call Framework

### 7.1 The Gap Selling Discovery Architecture (Keenan)

Once you've earned the meeting, the discovery call is where you create the gap.

**Structure:**

- Spend 45% of the call asking questions
- Rep speaks 20%, listens 80%
- 12–18 purposeful questions across a 20-minute call
- Set a structured agenda upfront

**Call Opening:**

> "Thanks for making the time. Here's what I'd like to do — I'd like to spend about 15 minutes understanding your current situation and challenges, and about 5 minutes on where you want things to be. If at the end it looks like there's a fit, we can talk about next steps. If not, no hard feelings. Sound good?"

### 7.2 Discovery Question Sequence

**Current State Questions:**

- "Walk me through how your team currently handles [process]."
- "What tools/systems are you using for [function]?"
- "You mentioned [symptom] — what does that actually look like day to day?"
- "How often does this happen — daily, weekly, per order?"

**Root Cause Questions:**

- "Why do you think this is happening?"
- "What have you tried to fix this, and why didn't it work?"
- "Is this a people problem, a process problem, or a tools problem?"

**Impact Questions:**

- "What does that cost you annually? In rework? In overtime? In lost customers?"
- "What opportunities are you missing because of this?"
- "What happens if this isn't solved in the next quarter?"

**Emotion Questions:**

- "How does this make you feel as the person responsible for it?"
- "What's at stake for you personally if this doesn't get resolved?"

**Future State Questions:**

- "If we solved [specific problem], what would that allow you to do that you can't do right now?"
- "How would you know this is working? What metrics would move?"
- "How would your team's day-to-day change?"

**Transition:**

> "So if I'm hearing you right — [problem] is happening [frequency], costing you roughly [impact], and the root cause is [X]. Does that sound accurate? So let me ask you this — what does it look like when that's fixed?"

### 7.3 The Voss Phone Techniques for Discovery

**Mirroring:** Repeat the last 2–3 words with upward inflection, then stay silent. Triggers elaboration.

**Labeling:** "It sounds like..." / "It seems like..." then go silent. Negative emotions diffuse when labeled.

**Calibrated Questions:** Use "what" and "how" only — never "why" (feels accusatory on phone).

### 7.4 Discovery Call Scorecard

| Element | Yes/No |
|---------|--------|
| Agenda set upfront? | |
| Problem uncovered and confirmed? | |
| Root cause identified? | |
| Impact quantified (in dollars, hours, or risk)? | |
| Emotion surfaced? | |
| Future state defined with metrics? | |
| Next step agreed with specific date/time? | |

## Part 8: Time Blocking & Daily Discipline

### 8.1 The Prospecting Block

**Minimum:** 4 hours/week in 90-minute to 2-hour blocks (Weinberg)

**Aggressive growth:** 16–18 hours/week — roughly one-third of total work time

**Golden Hours (Blount):** 8–9 AM and 4–6 PM for phone prospecting only. No email, no admin, no CRM during these blocks.

### 8.2 Daily Activity Framework

| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 7:00–7:30 AM | Pre-call research (review 10 target accounts, check LinkedIn, set objectives) |
| 7:30–9:00 AM | Hour of Power #1 — Execute 15 Triples (call + VM + email) |
| 9:00–10:00 AM | Discovery calls / meetings |
| 10:00–11:30 AM | Follow-up calls (callbacks, second-touch calls) |
| 11:30–12:00 PM | CRM updates, admin |
| 12:00–1:00 PM | LinkedIn engagement (comment on prospect posts, accept connections) |
| 1:00–3:00 PM | Meetings, demos, proposals |
| 3:00–4:00 PM | Pre-call research for tomorrow's targets |
| 4:00–5:30 PM | Hour of Power #2 — Execute 15 more Triples |

### 8.3 The "Go for No" Mindset (Fenton & Waltz)

Set a daily rejection QUOTA, not a success quota. "Get 20 no's today" removes emotional weight from each call. Every "we already have a supplier," "not interested," or voicemail counts as one. Keeps activity levels high during the 3–12 month manufacturing sales cycle.

## Part 9: Best Entry Strategies for Manufacturing

### 9.1 The Backup Source Positioning

Offer to become a backup/secondary source rather than asking them to switch. Removes threat, gets a foot in the door, positions you perfectly when the primary supplier has a bad quarter.

> "I'm not trying to replace your current supplier. What I'd like to do is get qualified as your backup — so when they're at capacity, or you need a second quote, or you want to test a new material, you already have someone who knows your specs. Can we spend 15 minutes getting me up to speed on your typical requirements?"

### 9.2 The Educational Approach (Chet Holmes — Dream 100)

Offer genuinely valuable industry research as the meeting hook.

> "We've been tracking the top 5 reasons manufacturing plants are losing margin this year — I'd like to walk you through the research and see if any of it resonates with what you're seeing at [Company]. It's about 20 minutes and worth your time either way."

### 9.3 The Referral Wedge (Ross — Predictable Revenue)

Email someone above your target and ask for a referral down. Then call the referred contact.

> "Hi [Name] — [VP Name] suggested I contact you directly about [specific topic]. She mentioned you'd be the right person to talk to about [function]. I'd love to schedule a quick call to understand your current setup."

### 9.4 The Challenger Reframe (Dixon & Adamson)

Lead with an insight that challenges their current thinking.

> "Most [role] I talk to in [industry] are focused on [obvious problem]. What the data actually shows is that [counterintuitive insight] has [X]x more impact on [metric]. That's the piece that's actually worth fixing."
